Referee skills beyond the rules

Positions, switches, preparation, game management, body language and composure: the part of the IHF observation report that a rules quiz does not test.

Each module starts from the items of the IHF Indoor Referee Performance Report that NRC evaluators use, so the training and the evaluation describe the same thing. Practical advice comes from the sources listed at the bottom. Where a point is common practice in referee courses rather than an IHF rule, it says so: agree it with your partner in the pre-game briefing.

1. Positions: court referee and goal referee

IHF report items: Referee cooperation and positioning (correct pick of position and running lanes); Struggling pivot and defender (goal referee observation).

  • Court referee. Behind the attacking team, watching defensive and offensive fouls by the court players and possession violations.
  • Goal referee. On the outer goal line, watching violations near the goal area and checking that the ball fully crossed the line for a goal.
  • Diagonal. The two stand diagonally so that each can watch one side line.
  • Split of the attack. A common split in national courses: the court referee takes the backs and the centre, the goal referee the wings and the pivot. Agree it before the game.
  • Pivot zone. Between the 6 and 9 metre lines most problems occur. The goal referee owns the pivot and defender duel; the court referee helps from the diagonal.

Practice. Watch the first ten minutes of a logged game with video, once as court referee and once as goal referee. Write down every call you would make from that position only.

2. Switching during the game

  • On every change of possession the roles switch: the court referee becomes the goal referee at the other end, and the reverse.
  • Changing sides. Referee courses advise moving to the other side of the court every few minutes so both referees cover all four corners. Agree when and how in the briefing.
  • Keep it smooth. A switch should not leave a corner unwatched or slow the restart. Use eye contact before you move.
  • Fast breaks. The new court referee follows the play; the new goal referee runs to the goal line first.

Practice. On a game video, count the switches in one half and note any where a referee arrived late on the goal line.

3. Pre-game preparation

IHF report item: Game management (pre-game, running match and after game phase).

  • Mind and body. Prepare mentally and physically. Learn about both teams and anything specific to the game, without preconceived ideas.
  • Checks. Court and goals (Rule 1), balls (Rule 3), team lists and uniforms (Rule 4).
  • Pair briefing. Who covers what, signals between you, how you switch, who whistles goals and 7 metre throws, how to handle a time-out and the last minute.
  • Table briefing. How the table alerts you, how suspensions and time-outs are signalled, who interrupts for a faulty substitution.

4. Game management

IHF report items: Reading the game, Advantage, Straight line, Progressive punishment, Game management.

  • Know the purpose of the rules, not only their text, and apply them with common sense.
  • Read the game: time calls so the game flows; whistle what matters, not every contact.
  • Advantage: same balance for both teams.
  • Straight line: clear, consistent calls so players, coaches and spectators see the line from the start.
  • Progressive punishment: a clear and strict line in accordance with the rules (Rule 16). A firm line early is easier to hold than one tightened late.

5. Body language and signals

IHF report item: Body language and signals (neutral, positive attitude, neither arrogant nor insecure; firm but not repressive in position, signals and gestures).

  • Use the IHF hand signals, clearly and in the same order every time.
  • Show the decision and, when useful, the reason. Good body language makes the call understandable to everyone around the court.
  • Stand still to signal; move to the next position after.
  • Vary the whistle: short for a normal foul, stronger for a serious one.

6. Composure and neutrality

IHF report items: Fair play and neutrality, Atmosphere.

  • Be modest but not submissive. Have the courage to intervene, take difficult decisions and stand by them.
  • Do not let players, coaches or spectators influence a decision.
  • Treat both teams and their officials the same way, in decisions and in tone.
  • After a mistake, reset at the next restart: the next call matters more than the last one.

7. Cooperation in the pair and with the table

IHF report items: Referee cooperation and positioning, Cooperation with the table.

  • Keep eye contact at every stoppage. Agree simple signals for "mine", "yours" and "I saw it".
  • If both whistle with different decisions, take the time to agree before restarting.
  • With the table: clear calls on every punishment and continuous contact. See the table officials guide.

8. Self-review after each game

  • Rate your own game on the 21 items of the IHF report, in the same six steps an evaluator uses.
  • When an evaluator's report is released on your Evaluations tab, compare it with your own rating. The gaps are your training plan.
  • Pick two points to work on before your next game, and tell your partner.
